\noindent Notation as in the Stochastic Processes lecture. $B$ is a standard Brownian motion, so $B_t$ has the $N(0,t)$ distribution. $\Prob_i$ and $\E_i$ refer to a chain started at state $i$. In the gambler's ruin, $p$ is the probability of winning a round, $q=1-p$, and $r=q/p$. $\PP=(p(i,j))$ is the transition matrix; row $i$ is the conditional pmf of the next state given the current state $i$. $\pi$ is a stationary distribution, $\pi\PP=\pi$. For a target set $A$, $h_i=\Prob_i(\text{reach }A)$ is the hitting probability and $k_i=\E_i[\text{number of steps until }A\text{ is reached}]$ is the expected hitting time; both satisfy the first-step systems of the lecture. $\Phi$ is the cdf of $N(0,1)$; the values needed below are
\[
\Phi(0.100)=0.540,\qquad\Phi(0.500)=0.691,\qquad\Phi(0.674)=0.750,\qquad\Phi(1.000)=0.841.
\]

\begin{enumerate}[resume*=quiz]
\item Hitting times of Brownian motion. The reflection principle states that for $a>0$ and $t>0$,
\[
\Prob\big(\max_{s\le t}B_s\ge a\big)=2\,\Prob(B_t\ge a).
\]
For $a>0$ let $T_a=\min\{s\ge0:B_s=a\}$ be the first time $B$ reaches the level $a$. The event $\{T_a\le t\}$ is the same as the event $\{\max_{s\le t}B_s\ge a\}$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),leftmargin=2em]
\item Compute $\Prob\big(\max_{s\le4}B_s\ge2\big)$.
\item Write $\Prob(T_1\le t)$ in terms of $\Phi$. Evaluate it at $t=1$, $t=4$ and $t=100$.
\item The median of $T_1$ is the value $t$ with $\Prob(T_1\le t)=\tfrac12$. Find it.
\item The scaling property of the lecture states that $\big(\tfrac1aB_{a^2t}\big)_{t\ge0}$ is again a Brownian motion. Use it to show that $T_a$ has the same distribution as $a^2T_1$. Give the median of $T_a$.
\item The lecture showed that $\E[T_1]=\infty$. Explain in at most three sentences how an infinite mean is consistent with the median found in (c). You may use the approximation $\Prob(T_1>t)\approx\sqrt{2/(\pi t)}$ for large $t$, from the lecture.
\end{enumerate}
\begin{solution}
(a) By the reflection principle, $\Prob(\max_{s\le4}B_s\ge2)=2\,\Prob(B_4\ge2)$. Since $B_4\sim N(0,4)$, $\Prob(B_4\ge2)=\Prob(N(0,1)\ge1)=1-\Phi(1)$. So the probability is $2(1-\Phi(1))$.
\ans{$\Prob(\max_{s\le4}B_s\ge2)=0.317$ (the three-place table value $\Phi(1)=0.841$ gives $0.318$; the values below are exact to three places).}

(b) $\Prob(T_1\le t)=\Prob(\max_{s\le t}B_s\ge1)=2\,\Prob(B_t\ge1)$. Since $B_t\sim N(0,t)$, $\Prob(B_t\ge1)=1-\Phi(1/\sqrt t)$. So
$\Prob(T_1\le t)=2\big(1-\Phi(1/\sqrt t)\big)$.
\ans{At $t=1$: $2(1-\Phi(1))=0.317$. At $t=4$: $2(1-\Phi(0.5))=0.617$. At $t=100$: $2(1-\Phi(0.1))=0.920$.}

(c) $2\big(1-\Phi(1/\sqrt t)\big)=\tfrac12$ holds if and only if $\Phi(1/\sqrt t)=\tfrac34$, that is $1/\sqrt t=0.674$, that is $t=1/0.674^2$.
\ans{The median of $T_1$ is $2.20$.}

(d) Let $\tilde B_t=\tfrac1aB_{a^2t}$, a Brownian motion by the scaling property. $\tilde B$ reaches $1$ at time $t$ exactly when $B$ reaches $a$ at time $a^2t$. So the first time $\tilde B$ reaches $1$ is $T_a/a^2$, and this time has the distribution of $T_1$ because $\tilde B$ is a Brownian motion. Hence $T_a$ has the distribution of $a^2T_1$.
\ans{The median of $T_a$ is $2.20\,a^2$.}

(e) Half of the paths reach $1$ by time $2.2$, but the waiting time of the other half has a heavy tail: $\Prob(T_1>t)=2\Phi(1/\sqrt t)-1\approx\sqrt{2/(\pi t)}$ decays only like $t^{-1/2}$. The mean is $\E[T_1]=\int_0^\infty\Prob(T_1>t)\dd t$, and $\int^\infty t^{-1/2}\dd t=\infty$. A median of $2.2$ and an infinite mean are consistent because the rare long waits are long enough to make the integral diverge.
% python3: 2(1-Phi(1))=0.3173; t=4: 0.6171; t=100: 0.9203; median 2.198
\end{solution}

\item Bold play. A gambler wins each round with probability $p=0.4$ and loses it with probability $q=0.6$, so $r=q/p=1.5$. The fortune starts at $\pounds3$ and the target is $\pounds10$; the game ends when the fortune reaches $\pounds0$ or $\pounds10$. Two strategies are compared.
Timid play stakes $\pounds1$ per round. From the lecture, with $N=10$ and starting fortune $i$,
\[
h_i=\frac{r^i-1}{r^N-1},\qquad
\E_i[T]=\frac{i}{q-p}-\frac{N}{q-p}\cdot\frac{1-r^i}{1-r^N},
\]
where $T$ is the number of rounds until the game ends.
Bold play stakes, from fortune $i$, the amount $\min(i,10-i)$: as much as possible without overshooting the target. A win adds the stake to the fortune and a loss removes it. So the fortune moves from $3$ to $6$ (win) or $0$ (loss); from $6$ to $10$ or $2$; from $2$ to $4$ or $0$; from $4$ to $8$ or $0$; from $8$ to $10$ or $6$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),leftmargin=2em]
\item Timid play: compute $h_3$ and $\E_3[T]$ from the two formulas above.
\item Bold play: draw the chain on the states $\{0,2,3,4,6,8,10\}$ with the transition probabilities. Write the first-step system for $h_i=\Prob_i(\text{reach }10)$.
\item Solve the system of (b) for $h_3$. Compare with the timid $h_3$ of (a).
\item Bold play: write the first-step system for $k_i=\E_i[\text{number of bets until the game ends}]$ and solve it for $k_3$. Compare with the timid $\E_3[T]$ of (a).
\item Now let the game be fair, $p=q=\tfrac12$. Solve the bold-play system of (b) for $h_3$. For timid play the lecture gives $h_3=\tfrac3{10}$. Explain in at most three sentences why the two values agree.
\end{enumerate}
\begin{solution}
(a) With $r=1.5$: $h_3=\frac{1.5^3-1}{1.5^{10}-1}=\frac{2.375}{56.665}=0.042$. And $\E_3[T]=\frac{3}{0.2}-\frac{10}{0.2}\cdot\frac{1-1.5^3}{1-1.5^{10}}=15-50\cdot\frac{-2.375}{-56.665}=15-2.10=12.9$.
\ans{Timid play from $\pounds3$: $h_3=0.042$ and $\E_3[T]=12.9$ rounds.}

(b) The chain: $3\to6$ with probability $p$, $3\to0$ with probability $q$; $6\to10$ ($p$), $6\to2$ ($q$); $2\to4$ ($p$), $2\to0$ ($q$); $4\to8$ ($p$), $4\to0$ ($q$); $8\to10$ ($p$), $8\to6$ ($q$); $0$ and $10$ are absorbing. The first-step system is
\ans{$h_0=0$, $h_{10}=1$, $h_3=ph_6$, $h_6=p+qh_2$, $h_2=ph_4$, $h_4=ph_8$, $h_8=p+qh_6$.}

(c) From $h_2=ph_4=p^2h_8$ we get $h_6=p+qp^2h_8$, and then $h_8=p+qh_6=p+qp+q^2p^2h_8$. So
$h_8=\frac{p(1+q)}{1-q^2p^2}=\frac{0.4\cdot1.6}{1-0.0576}=\frac{0.64}{0.9424}=\frac{400}{589}=0.679$.
Then $h_6=p+qp^2h_8=\frac{274}{589}=0.465$, $h_4=ph_8=\frac{160}{589}=0.272$, $h_2=ph_4=\frac{64}{589}=0.109$, and $h_3=ph_6=\frac{548}{2945}$.
\ans{Bold play from $\pounds3$: $h_3=0.186$, about four and a half times the timid $0.042$.}

(d) Each bet costs one step, so
$k_0=k_{10}=0$, $k_3=1+pk_6$, $k_6=1+qk_2$, $k_2=1+pk_4$, $k_4=1+pk_8$, $k_8=1+qk_6$.
Substituting $k_8$ into $k_4$, then into $k_2$, then into $k_6$: $k_6=1+q+qp+qp^2+q^2p^2k_6$, so $k_6=\frac{1+q+qp+qp^2}{1-q^2p^2}=\frac{1.936}{0.9424}=\frac{1210}{589}$, and $k_3=1+pk_6=1+\frac{484}{589}=\frac{1073}{589}$.
\ans{Bold play from $\pounds3$: $k_3=1.82$ bets, against $12.9$ rounds of timid play.}

(e) With $p=q=\tfrac12$ the formula of (c) gives $h_8=\frac{\frac12\cdot\frac32}{1-\frac1{16}}=\frac45$, then $h_2=\tfrac14h_8=\tfrac15$, $h_6=\tfrac12+\tfrac12\cdot\tfrac15=\tfrac35$ and $h_3=\tfrac12h_6=\tfrac3{10}$.
\ans{Bold play in the fair game: $h_3=\tfrac3{10}$, the same as timid play.}
Every round of a fair game has expected gain $0$ whatever the stake, so the expected fortune when the game ends equals the starting fortune: $\E[X_T]=3$. The final fortune is $10$ with probability $h_3$ and $0$ otherwise, so $\E[X_T]=10h_3$. Hence $h_3=\tfrac3{10}$ for every strategy that stops only at $0$ or $10$.
% python3 sympy: bold h = (64,548/5,160,274,400)/589 for i=2,3,4,6,8 -> h3=548/2945=0.1861; k3=1073/589=1.822; timid 0.04191, 12.90; fair bold h3=3/10
\end{solution}

\item PageRank. A random surfer on $n$ pages moves as follows: with probability $d=0.85$ it follows a uniformly chosen link out of the current page, and with probability $1-d=0.15$ it jumps to a uniformly chosen page. Its transition matrix is
\[
\PP=d\,\mathbf H+\frac{1-d}{n}\,\mathbf J,
\]
where $\mathbf H_{ij}$ is $1/(\text{number of links out of }i)$ if page $i$ links to page $j$ and $0$ otherwise, and $\mathbf J$ is the matrix of ones. The PageRank of a page is its entry in the stationary distribution $\pi$ of $\PP$.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),leftmargin=2em]
\item Three pages with links $\st A\to\st B$, $\st A\to\st C$, $\st B\to\st C$, $\st C\to\st A$. Write $\mathbf H$ and $\PP=0.85\,\mathbf H+0.05\,\mathbf J$.
\item Solve $\pi\PP=\pi$, $\pi_{\st A}+\pi_{\st B}+\pi_{\st C}=1$, and rank the three pages.
\item Pages $\st A$ and $\st B$ each have a single in-link. Explain in at most two sentences why $\pi_{\st A}>\pi_{\st B}$.
\item Find the stationary distribution of $\mathbf H$ itself, that is, without damping ($d=1$).
\item Add a fourth page $\st D$ with the links $\st C\to\st D$ (so $\st C$ now links to both $\st A$ and $\st D$) and $\st D\to\st D$, and no other links out of $\st D$. Find the stationary distribution without damping ($d=1$) and with $d=0.85$ (now $n=4$, so $\PP=0.85\,\mathbf H+0.0375\,\mathbf J$).
\item Describe in at most three sentences what the page $\st D$ does to the surfer when $d=1$, and what the damping changes.
\end{enumerate}
\begin{solution}
(a) $\st A$ has two out-links, $\st B$ and $\st C$ one each:
\ans{$\mathbf H=\begin{pmatrix}0&\tfrac12&\tfrac12\\0&0&1\\1&0&0\end{pmatrix}$, so $\PP=\begin{pmatrix}0.05&0.475&0.475\\0.05&0.05&0.9\\0.9&0.05&0.05\end{pmatrix}$.}

(b) The columns of $\pi\PP=\pi$ read
$\pi_{\st A}=0.05+0.85\pi_{\st C}$, $\pi_{\st B}=0.05+0.425\pi_{\st A}$, $\pi_{\st C}=0.05+0.425\pi_{\st A}+0.85\pi_{\st B}$
(using $\pi_{\st A}+\pi_{\st B}+\pi_{\st C}=1$ to collect the $0.05$ terms). Substituting the first two into the third and solving,
$\pi=(\tfrac{686}{1769},\tfrac{380}{1769},\tfrac{703}{1769})=(0.388,0.215,0.397)$.
\ans{Ranking: $\st C$ first, $\st A$ second, $\st B$ third.}

(c) The in-link of $\st A$ is the only link out of $\st C$, the top-ranked page, so $\st A$ receives all of $\st C$'s link mass. The in-link of $\st B$ is one of two links out of $\st A$, so $\st B$ receives only half of $\st A$'s link mass.

(d) $\pi\mathbf H=\pi$ reads $\pi_{\st A}=\pi_{\st C}$, $\pi_{\st B}=\tfrac12\pi_{\st A}$, $\pi_{\st C}=\tfrac12\pi_{\st A}+\pi_{\st B}$, with $\pi_{\st A}+\pi_{\st B}+\pi_{\st C}=1$.
\ans{Without damping, $\pi=(\tfrac25,\tfrac15,\tfrac25)$.}

(e) Without damping, $\st D$ is an absorbing state: $\mathbf H_{\st D\st D}=1$. The surfer returns to $\st C$ repeatedly, and on each visit it moves to $\st D$ with probability $\tfrac12$, so it reaches $\st D$ with probability $1$ and then never leaves. The only stationary distribution is $\pi=(0,0,0,1)$.
\ans{With $d=0.85$, solving $\pi\PP=\pi$ with $\sum\pi=1$ gives $\pi=(0.101,0.080,0.148,0.671)$ for $(\st A,\st B,\st C,\st D)$.}

(f) When $d=1$, $\st D$ is absorbing: the surfer eventually enters $\st D$ and stays there, and every other page has rank $0$. With damping, each visit to $\st D$ lasts a geometric number of steps with mean $1/0.15=6.7$, and each random jump lands on $\st D$ with probability $\tfrac14$, so $\st D$ still holds two thirds of the mass. Damping keeps every rank positive but does not remove the advantage of a self-linking page; search engines treat such pages separately.

\end{solution}

\item Tennis at deuce. From deuce, the server wins each point with probability $p$ and loses it with probability $q=1-p$, independently of the other points. The game is won by the first player to lead by two points. The states are $\st D$ (deuce), $\st I$ (advantage server), $\st O$ (advantage receiver), $\st W$ (game won by the server) and $\st L$ (game lost by the server). From $\st D$ the next state is $\st I$ or $\st O$; from $\st I$ it is $\st W$ or $\st D$; from $\st O$ it is $\st D$ or $\st L$; $\st W$ and $\st L$ are absorbing.
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\alph*),leftmargin=2em]
\item Write the transition matrix, with the states in the order $\st D,\st I,\st O,\st W,\st L$.
\item Write the first-step system for $h_i=\Prob_i(\text{reach }\st W)$. Solve it and show that $h_{\st D}=\dfrac{p^2}{p^2+q^2}$.
\item Write the first-step system for $k_i=\E_i[\text{number of points until the game ends}]$. Solve it and show that $k_{\st D}=\dfrac{2}{1-2pq}$.
\item Evaluate $h_{\st D}$ and $k_{\st D}$ for $p=0.5$, $0.55$, $0.6$ and $0.7$. For $p=0.55$, state $h_{\st D}$ as a percentage.
\item Let $m\ge1$. The game ends after exactly $2m$ further points if the first $m-1$ pairs of points return to deuce and the $m$-th pair does not. Write $h_{\st D}$ as an infinite series by summing over $m$. Then explain in at most two sentences why the first-step system of (b) gives $h_{\st D}$ without summing a series.
\end{enumerate}
\begin{solution}
(a) In the order $\st D,\st I,\st O,\st W,\st L$:
\ans{$\PP=\begin{pmatrix}0&p&q&0&0\\q&0&0&p&0\\p&0&0&0&q\\0&0&0&1&0\\0&0&0&0&1\end{pmatrix}$.}

(b) $h_{\st W}=1$, $h_{\st L}=0$, $h_{\st D}=ph_{\st I}+qh_{\st O}$, $h_{\st I}=p+qh_{\st D}$, $h_{\st O}=ph_{\st D}$.
Substituting the last two equations into the third: $h_{\st D}=p(p+qh_{\st D})+q\,ph_{\st D}=p^2+2pq\,h_{\st D}$, so $h_{\st D}(1-2pq)=p^2$. Since $p+q=1$, $1-2pq=(p+q)^2-2pq=p^2+q^2$.
\ans{$h_{\st D}=\dfrac{p^2}{p^2+q^2}$. For $p=0.6$: $h_{\st D}=\tfrac9{13}$, $h_{\st I}=\tfrac{57}{65}$, $h_{\st O}=\tfrac{27}{65}$.}

(c) $k_{\st W}=k_{\st L}=0$, $k_{\st D}=1+pk_{\st I}+qk_{\st O}$, $k_{\st I}=1+qk_{\st D}$, $k_{\st O}=1+pk_{\st D}$.
Substituting: $k_{\st D}=1+p(1+qk_{\st D})+q(1+pk_{\st D})=1+p+q+2pq\,k_{\st D}=2+2pq\,k_{\st D}$.
\ans{$k_{\st D}=\dfrac2{1-2pq}$. For $p=0.6$: $k_{\st D}=\tfrac{50}{13}=3.85$ points.}

(d) For $p=0.5,\ 0.55,\ 0.6,\ 0.7$: $h_{\st D}=0.5,\ 0.599,\ 0.692,\ 0.845$ and $k_{\st D}=4,\ 3.96,\ 3.85,\ 3.45$.
\ans{For $p=0.55$, the server wins the game from deuce with probability $60\%$: a $55/45$ edge per point becomes a $60/40$ edge per game, because the game ends only when one player is two points ahead.}

(e) A pair of points returns to deuce with probability $2pq$ (one point each, in either order) and ends the game with probability $p^2+q^2$, of which $p^2$ is a win for the server. So the server wins after exactly $2m$ points with probability $(2pq)^{m-1}p^2$, and
\ans{$h_{\st D}=\sum_{m\ge1}(2pq)^{m-1}p^2=\dfrac{p^2}{1-2pq}$, the same value as in (b).}
The first-step system uses the return to deuce to write $h_{\st D}$ in terms of itself, in the equation $h_{\st D}=p^2+2pq\,h_{\st D}$. Solving this one equation performs the summation of the geometric series implicitly.
% python3 sympy: p=0.6: h=(9/13,57/65,27/65), k=(50/13,33/13,43/13); table 0.5/4, 0.5990/3.9604, 0.6923/3.8462, 0.8448/3.4483
\end{solution}
\end{enumerate}
